Ben and the Sea That Holds You Up

Ben is four years old, and today he is going to see a sea he has never seen before.

He drives there with Dad, down a long road between big round hills the colour of sand.

The road goes down, and down, and down, until Dad laughs and says they cannot go any lower.

This sea lies at the very lowest place on the whole round world, and it is waiting for them at the bottom of the hill.

When Ben climbs out of the car, the shore is white and shiny, and he is quite sure that it is snow.

But the white is not cold at all. It is warm, and hard, and bumpy under his two bare feet.

"It is salt," says Dad. "There is so much salt in this water that it climbs out and sits on the stones."

Ben touches one white lump with one careful finger, and it crunches under his hand like sugar.

Dad walks into the water and turns around. "Come in, Ben," he calls. "The water is warm."

But Ben stays on the shore, holding a big smooth stone with both hands, and he does not move at all.

In the swimming pool at home, Ben always goes down. His feet go down. His chin goes down.

"I will sink," says Ben, in a very small voice. "I am too heavy for the water."

Dad comes back out and kneels down beside him, so that their two faces are at the very same height.

"Hold out your hand," says Dad, and he pours a little pile of white salt into it.

"Water with salt in it is heavier than water without salt, and heavy water pushes up hard."

"This sea is so full of salt that it will push you up too. It will hold you, like a hand under your back."

Salt in the water. Salt in the sea. Up, up, up — the water holds me.

Ben puts in one toe. Then one foot. Then both feet, right up to his two round knees.

The water is warm, and slippery, and a little bit oily, like the smooth inside of a shell.

He sits down very slowly, exactly the way he sits down in the bath at home.

And then something funny happens: both of his legs float straight up in front of him, all by themselves.

Ben laughs out loud, because he never told his legs to do that.

"Lie back," says Dad. "I am right here beside you." And Ben lies back on the water, very carefully.

The water holds his head. It holds his arms. It holds his legs and his round little tummy.

Ben is floating. He is lying on top of a whole sea, like one leaf lying on top of a puddle.

Salt in the water. Salt in the sea. Up, up, up — the water holds me.

Ben looks down into the water, right next to his own two floating feet, and waits for a fish to swim past.

No fish comes. Not one.

"Nothing big can live in here," says Dad. "For a fish, this water is far too salty."

"That is why people call it the Dead Sea. But look at us, Ben — we can float in it beautifully."

Ben floats, and he does not mind the quiet water one little bit.

The sun goes down behind the big hills on the other side, and the whole sea turns pink.

Then the water turns orange. Then it turns a soft, sleepy grey.

Dad floats along beside him with his hands behind his head, humming a little tune.

Ben floats and floats, and his arms are loose and easy, and he is not frightened any more.

Salt in the water. Salt in the sea. Up, up, up — the water holds me.

Dad washes all the salt off Ben with clean fresh water, and wraps him up in a big soft towel.

In the car, Ben leans on the cool window and watches the road go up, and up, and up.

His eyes close. He can still feel the warm water holding him up, all the way along his back.

Salt in the water. Salt in the sea. Up, up, up — the water holds me.

Before we sleep

  1. What would you do first, if you could float on top of the water all by yourself?
  2. Ben was afraid he would sink. What helps you when something new feels a bit scary?

Tomorrow you can fill a tall glass with water and lower an egg in with a spoon: it sinks straight to the bottom. Then stir in spoon after spoon of salt, and watch the egg climb up and float, exactly the way Ben floated.

Good night

The water is dark and still now, the hills are quiet, and your bed is holding you up until morning.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

The Dead Sea, on the border between Israel and Jordan, holds roughly 290-300 grams of dissolved salt in every kilogram of water. The open ocean holds about 35 grams, so this water is close to nine times as salty. All that dissolved salt makes the water much heavier for its size, and a floating body sinks only until the water it pushes aside weighs as much as the body does - which here happens almost at once. That is why a person lies on the surface with most of the body still out of the water, and why Ben's legs pop up before he even lies back.

The rest of the picture is real too. Salt really does crust the shore white and crunchy, the water really does feel slippery and oily (that is the magnesium salts), and no fish or other large animals live in the lake - only salt-loving microbes - which is exactly where the name comes from. The surface of the Dead Sea is the lowest land surface anywhere on Earth, more than 400 metres below sea level, and it is dropping by about a metre every year.

One thing the story quietly leaves out, on purpose: the water stings sharply in the eyes and in any small cut, and it must never be swallowed. In real life a four-year-old floats there only in shallow water with a grown-up's hands right there, which is the one part of Ben's evening worth saying out loud before anybody plans a trip.
