Leo and Saturn's Quiet Rings

Leo is four years old, and he has one favourite toy in the whole wide world.

It is a little planet, gold and round, with a flat ring going all the way around its middle.

Every night, when Dad switches off the big light, Leo spins the little ring with one finger and sings to it.

Round and round and round they go. Quiet, quiet, soft and slow.

But tonight the little planet slipped right out of Leo's hand and bounced away under the bed.

He heard one small tick as it hit the wall, and then everything went very still.

When Dad pulled it out, the gold ring had come off, and there was a small crack running across it.

Leo's mouth went wobbly. "Dad," he said, "if my ring can break, can the real one break too?"

Dad sat down on the edge of the bed, and he thought about it for a long, quiet moment.

"Leo," said Dad, "I have a funny thing to tell you. The real ring is already broken."

Leo sat straight up. That was not what he thought Dad was going to say at all.

"It is not one smooth piece, like yours," said Dad. "It is billions and billions of little pieces."

"Pieces of ice. Cold, shiny ice, floating around and around out there in the dark."

Leo turned his cracked little toy over and over in his hands, trying to picture it.

Dad found an orange in the kitchen and set it down in the middle of a big dark tray.

Then he poured a thin line of sugar, slowly, in a great big circle all the way around it.

"Look very close," he said. "A thousand tiny grains. Now stand back, and look again."

Leo stepped back, and the grains were not grains any more. They were one smooth white ring.

Round and round and round they go. Quiet, quiet, soft and slow.

"Are the ice pieces little, like the sugar?" asked Leo.

"Some of them are," said Dad. "Some are smaller than the dust on the end of your finger."

"Some are like snowballs. Some are as big as our whole house. And a few are as big as a mountain."

"And every single one of them goes around Saturn all by itself, like its own tiny moon."

Leo tried to imagine a whole mountain made of ice, floating quietly high above his head.

"Is the ring fat?" asked Leo, holding his two hands far, far apart.

Dad laughed, very softly. "It is wide. So wide you could lay the whole world across it, again and again."

"But it is so thin that if you floated round to the side, the ring would almost vanish."

"Like a paper plate," said Leo. "A giant paper plate, made out of ice."

He squeezed his two hands as flat together as he could, and his fingers still looked fat.

Leo pressed his nose against the cold dark window and looked up at the sky.

"Is it loud out there?" he whispered. "All those pieces of ice, bumping into each other?"

"No," said Dad. "Out there, there is no air at all. And a sound needs air to travel."

"So the whole enormous ring turns, and turns, and nobody hears a single thing."

Round and round and round they go. Quiet, quiet, soft and slow.

Dad found a little piece of tape, and the gold ring went back onto the toy planet.

Leo stood it on the shelf beside his bed, where he could see it from his pillow.

Outside the window the sky was black and soft and full of tiny quiet lights.

He shut his eyes and thought about all that ice, turning slowly in the dark, far, far away.

Round and round and round they go. Quiet, quiet, soft and slow. Good night, Leo.

Let's listen: music for the planets

Long ago in England there was a man called Gustav Holst, and he wrote a piece of music for every planet.

This one is the softest of them all. It moves very slowly, a little like ice floating in the dark.

Close your eyes and listen. Can you hear the pieces going round?

Good night

Far away in the dark the ice goes round and round, and here in your bed it is warm and still.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

Saturn's rings really are not solid. They are made of countless separate pieces, almost entirely water ice with a little rock and dust mixed in, and every piece travels on its own orbit around the planet, exactly as Dad says. The sizes in the story are the ones NASA gives: from grains smaller than dust, through pebbles and snowballs, up to boulders the size of a house, with a few chunks as large as a mountain.

Wide and thin is right too. The ring system reaches roughly 280,000 km out from Saturn, while the main rings are only about ten metres thick in most places (some estimates run up to about a hundred metres) — so "a giant paper plate made of ice" is a fair picture for a four-year-old. And there really is no sound: space has no air to carry it, so the pieces jostle in complete silence.

One thing the story simplifies: you cannot see the rings from a window with your eyes alone. Saturn looks like a small steady yellow star, and Leo is imagining the rest. Even cheap binoculars held very still, or any small telescope, will show the ring as two little "ears" beside the planet.

Sources: NASA – Cassini: Saturn's Rings · NASA – Saturn Facts · Britannica – Saturn: The ring system
