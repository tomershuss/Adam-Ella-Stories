Ben and the Green That Was Hiding

Ben was four. The best time of his day was bedtime, when the room was warm and quiet and nobody was in a hurry.

On his little table he had one white paper, one soft brush, and three small jars of paint.

Red, like a tomato. Yellow, like the moon. Blue, like the sea.

Ben put his brush in the water and stirred it round and round and round.

Swish, swish. Swirl, swirl. Come out, colour, come out!

That morning, in the garden, Ben had found a frog.

It sat on a big wet leaf and blinked at him, slowly, as if it were almost asleep.

The frog was green. Green like grass. Green like a leaf after the rain.

"I will paint you tonight," Ben told the frog. "Then you can sleep on my wall."

Back in his room, Ben opened the first jar and looked inside. Red.

He opened the second jar. Yellow.

He opened the third jar. Blue.

No green. Not one drop of green, anywhere.

Ben put down his brush. A red frog is not a frog. A blue frog is not a frog either.

"Grandma!" Ben called. "My green is missing."

Grandma came in slowly, in her red cardigan, with her soft grey hair up in a bun.

She sat down next to Ben and looked at the three jars for a long, long time.

Then she smiled. "Your green is not missing," she said. "Your green is hiding."

"Hiding?" said Ben. "Where?"

"Inside the other colours," said Grandma. "Put some blue on your plate."

Ben painted a fat blue blob, round and shiny, in the middle of the white plate.

"Now put the yellow right on top of it," said Grandma.

Ben dropped a fat yellow blob on the blue, and he stirred, and stirred, and stirred.

Swish, swish. Swirl, swirl. Come out, colour, come out!

And then the blue and the yellow were gone, and something new was there instead.

Green! A soft, wet, shiny green, right in the middle of the plate.

"It was in there all the time!" said Ben. His eyes were very big.

"Blue and yellow, together, always make green," said Grandma. "Now you can paint your frog."

Ben painted a round green head, and two round green eyes on top of it.

He painted four green legs, one toe at a time, and a wide green smile.

The frog sat in the middle of the paper and looked right back at him.

"Hello, frog," Ben whispered. "I found your colour."

"My frog needs a sun," said Ben. "But a sun is not red, and a sun is not yellow."

"Then ask them both," said Grandma, and she gave him a clean corner of the plate.

Red first. Yellow on top. Round and round and round again.

Swish, swish. Swirl, swirl. Come out, colour, come out!

Orange! Ben painted a big orange sun in the corner, warm and round and happy.

"And now the night," said Ben, "because my frog is sleepy too."

He put a little red on the plate, and a little blue on the red, and he mixed them slowly.

Swish, swish. Swirl, swirl. Come out, colour, come out!

Purple. A quiet purple sky came out, and Ben painted it over the whole top of the page.

Grandma hung the painting on the wall, right above Ben's pillow, where he could see it.

The green frog sat in the grass, under an orange sun and a soft purple sky.

The jars were closed. The brush was clean. The room was warm and quiet again.

"Tomorrow," Ben yawned, "I will look for more colours hiding."

Swish, swish. Swirl, swirl. Good night, colours, good night.

Before we sleep

  1. If you had only red, yellow and blue paint, which colour would you try to make first?
  2. Can you find three green things in your room, without getting out of bed?

Tomorrow you can put one drop of blue and one drop of yellow on a white plate, add a little water, and stir them with a spoon. Watch the green come out. Then try red and yellow, and after that red and blue.

Good night

The paints are closed, the frog is asleep on the wall, and the purple sky is over your bed too.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

In painting, red, yellow and blue are the traditional primary colours: you cannot mix them out of other paints, and mixing two of them gives the secondary colours. Yellow and blue make green, red and yellow make orange, red and blue make violet (purple) — exactly the three mixes Ben makes.

Why it works: paint does not add light, it takes light away. A pigment absorbs part of the light that falls on it and reflects the rest, which is the colour we see. Mix two pigments and the mixture absorbs everything that either one absorbed, so only the narrow band both of them reflect is left over — green, from a blue and a yellow. This is called subtractive mixing, and it is also why a mixed colour is always a little darker and duller than the pure colours, and why mixing all of them gives a muddy brown rather than black.

The simplification: red-yellow-blue is the painter's tradition, not a law of nature. Printers use cyan, magenta and yellow, which reach many more colours, and with real tube paints the green you get depends on which blue and which yellow you started from. Mixing coloured light works the other way round: red, green and blue light together make white.
