Pip and the Wobbly Tower

Pip is a little mouse, small and grey, with round ears and a long thin tail.

She lives in a garden shed, in a soft box under the shelf where the flowerpots sleep.

High up in the wall there is a small round window, and at night the moon sits inside it.

Pip wants to see the moon up close, but the window is very high and Pip is very small.

So she says to herself, in her bravest voice: I will build a tower.

Pip has a pile of little sticks and a ball of soft brown string.

She ties one stick to another, corner to corner to corner to corner.

Now she has a square, standing on the floor like a small empty room.

This is the bottom of my tower, says Pip, and she is very proud of it.

She gives the square a little push, just to say hello.

The square does not stay a square.

It leans over to one side, it goes slanty like a slice of cake, and then it lies down flat on the floor.

Pip stands it up again, and again it slides down and down, until it is a puddle of sticks.

Wibble, wobble, wibble, wobble. Hold still, little tower, hold still.

Pip will not give up, so she ties one square, and another square, and another square, one on top of the other.

Now it is a tower, as tall as Pip, and then taller than Pip, and then taller than the watering can.

She puts one paw on the bottom stick and begins to climb.

The tower leans to the left, then it leans to the right, then it folds down slowly, all the way to the floor.

Pip sits down in the middle of her sticks and does not want to build any more.

Someone is standing in the shed door, with the moon behind him and a pencil behind his ear.

It is Grandpa Mo, and he sits down beside Pip, and Pip tells him what her tower does.

Wibble, wobble, wibble, wobble. Hold still, little tower, hold still.

Grandpa Mo does not say oh dear, he just picks up three sticks and ties them, corner to corner to corner.

Now there is a triangle in his paw, and when he pushes it hard, it does not move at all.

Grandpa Mo puts the square in one of Pip's paws and the triangle in the other.

Push them both, he says, and Pip pushes.

The square goes soft and slanty under her paw, but the triangle stays exactly the same.

A square can change its shape, says Grandpa Mo, but a triangle cannot change at all, not unless a stick gets longer or shorter.

Pip pushes the triangle again, and again, and again, and it is still a triangle.

Pip looks at her floppy square for a long time, and then she picks up one more stick.

She lays it across the square, from this corner all the way over to that corner.

Now the square is not a square any more, it is two triangles sitting side by side.

Pip pushes it, and nothing leans, and nothing slides, and nothing wobbles.

Wibble, wobble, wibble, wobble. Hold still, little tower, hold still.

Pip builds her tower all over again: a square, a stick across, a square, a stick across.

It grows up past the flowerpots, past the old watering can, past the shelf, up to the little round window.

Pip climbs, and the tower does not lean, and the tower does not fold.

Grandpa Mo holds the bottom and watches her go up, and up, and up.

It just stands there, still and strong, and lets her go all the way to the top.

At the top Pip sits on the window ledge, and there is the moon, big and round and quiet.

Far away over the dark garden stands a tall crane, and Pip sees that it is full of triangles too.

Grandpa Mo climbs up and sits beside her, and for a long while nobody says anything at all.

Then Pip goes down, slowly, and curls up in her soft box, and her tower stands still in the dark all night.

Wibble, wobble, wibble, wobble. Hold still, little tower, hold still.

Before we sleep

  1. What would you build if you had a big pile of sticks and a ball of string?
  2. Pip's tower fell down two times before it stood up. What do you do when something you build falls down?

Tomorrow you can build Pip's two shapes. Tape four drinking straws into a square and three straws into a triangle, then push the top of each one sideways with one finger and see which one changes shape. Now tape one more straw across the square, from corner to corner, and push it again.

Good night

The moon is in the little round window, the tower is standing still, and Pip is fast asleep.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

Grandpa Mo's rule is real geometry, and it is the reason Pip's first tower falls down. A triangle is the only shape whose form is fixed by the lengths of its sides: give it three sticks and the angles have no choice left. A four-stick frame with loose corners does have a choice - it can lean over into a slanted diamond without any stick getting longer or shorter - and that is exactly what Pip's square does.

Laying one stick across the square, corner to corner, turns it into two triangles and takes that choice away. Engineers call the result a truss, and once you have seen it you see it everywhere: crane booms, roof frames, the sides of bridges, electricity pylons, and the bracing hidden inside tall buildings.

One honest stretch: a square taped together from real straws does not flop as fast as Pip's does, because tape is not a perfect hinge and the straws themselves resist bending a little. A real tower also needs a wide base and strong joints, not only triangles. If you try the straw experiment, push the top corner sideways rather than pressing straight down, and the difference between the square and the triangle is easy to feel.
