Nina and the Sleepy Violin

Nina is four years old, and Bo is her bear, and they go to sleep together every single night.

Bo is soft and brown, and one of his ears folds down over his eye like a little brown leaf.

But tonight Bo will not sleep, and his round black eyes stay open, and open, and open.

So Nina sings him a song, the loudest and fastest song she knows, right into his folded ear.

Bo does not close his eyes even a tiny bit. He only looks and looks at Nina.

Grandpa hears the singing from the hall, and he comes in quietly, in his soft grey socks.

He is carrying a small brown case, no bigger than a cat that is fast asleep.

He puts it down on the blanket and opens it, and inside, on soft purple cloth, lies a little violin.

"This violin was mine when I was small like you," says Grandpa. "It knows a lot of songs."

Nina touches it with one careful finger, and four thin strings shine at her in the lamp light.

Grandpa shows Nina how to hold the bow, light as a feather, and how to pull it across the strings.

Nina pulls, and a bright note jumps out of the violin, high and quick, like a bird in the morning.

Bo's eyes open wider than ever, and he sits straight up against the pillow.

"That is a morning song," says Grandpa. "Bo is waiting for a night song."

"Then where do I find a night song?" asks Nina, holding the bow very still.

Grandpa takes Nina's finger and puts it down on one string, close to the bow.

"Only a piece of the string is singing," he says. "The piece between your finger and the bow."

Nina pulls the bow, and because that piece is short, out comes a small high note, like a bell on a cat.

Then Grandpa slides her finger far away from the bow, all the way up near the top.

Now the singing piece is long, and the note comes out low and round and slow, like a big soft yawn.

"There," says Grandpa, and his glasses shine. "Now you know the secret that every string in the world keeps."

Long string, low song. Short string, high song. Hmmmm.

Nina slides her finger close to the bow, and the note climbs up, up, high into the ceiling.

She slides it far away again, and the note walks back down, low and soft, into the blanket.

She does it again and again, and Bo blinks one long, slow blink.

Now Nina looks at all four strings together: one is thin like a hair, and one is fat like a piece of wool.

"The fat one has the deepest voice of them all," says Grandpa. "Try that one."

Nina puts her finger far from the bow, on the fat string, and pulls the bow as slowly as she can.

Out comes a low, soft hum, so quiet and so warm that the whole room stops to listen.

Long string, low song. Short string, high song. Hmmmm.

"That one," whispers Nina. "That is Bo's song, the one he was waiting for."

She plays it again, low and slow, and then again, even slower and softer than before.

Bo's round eyes go small, and then smaller, and then they are just two little lines.

His folded ear droops right down, and his head tips over onto the cool white pillow.

Grandpa does not say one single word. He only smiles, and nods, very slowly.

Grandpa lays the little violin back on its purple cloth and closes the brown case without a sound.

Nina climbs under her blanket and tucks Bo in beside her, right under her chin, where he belongs.

Outside the window the sky is dark blue, and one small star is still awake.

Nina hums the low song to herself, slower, and slower, and slower.

Long string, low song. Short string, high song. Hmmmm.

Let's listen: The Swan

A cello is like a violin that grew very big, and its strings are about twice as long.

So the cello sings low and slow and soft, just like the song that Nina found for Bo.

Close your eyes and listen, and see if you can hear a swan floating on quiet water.

Before we sleep

  1. What would you do to help Bo fall asleep, if there was no violin in the house?
  2. Which sounds in our house are high, and which sounds are low?

Tomorrow you can make a string of your own. Stretch a rubber band around an open box and pluck it. Press one finger on the band so the singing piece is short, then move your finger away so it is long, and listen to the song go up and down.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

The true part: the longer the vibrating piece of a string, the lower the note. Halve that length and the pitch jumps a whole octave. A finger pressed on a violin string does exactly this – it shortens the piece that is free to vibrate, so the note goes up. One small simplification: the vibrating piece really runs from the finger to the bridge (the little wooden saddle the strings cross), and the bow is drawn over that piece, so "between your finger and the bow" points at the right thing without being precise.

Length is not the only thing that matters, and the story is careful about this. A violin's four strings are all the same length; the low one sounds low because it is thicker and heavier, and the thin one sounds high. Tension counts too – tightening a string raises its pitch, which is what tuning pegs are for.

A cello's strings really are about twice as long as a violin's (roughly 70 cm against 33 cm), and they are thicker as well, which is a large part of why a cello sounds so much deeper. Nina's violin is a child's instrument, which is genuinely smaller than a grown-up's; a four-year-old would usually start on a 1/16 or 1/10 size.
