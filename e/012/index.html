Miko the Bat and the Song in the Dark

Miko was a small bat with soft brown fur and two big round ears.

He lived in a cave with his mama, high up near the stony roof.

All day long they slept upside down, wrapped in their wings like little brown coats.

Miko loved the cave in the daytime, when it was full of warm, sleepy bats.

That evening the cave filled with the sound of wings. Whoosh, whoosh, whoosh.

One by one the bats flew out of the doorway and into the dark blue night.

Miko stayed behind, holding the stone roof tight with his small strong feet.

Outside, the moths were dancing in the moonlight, and he could smell the warm night air.

The cave went very quiet. Only Mama stayed with him.

"Come, Miko," said Mama. "The night is warm, and the moths are dancing."

"I can't," said Miko. "The cave is too dark. I cannot see where to go."

He let go of the roof and flapped two little flaps. Bonk. His wing touched a cold, wet rock.

Miko climbed back up, held on tight, and hid his face in his wing.

A small tear ran down his furry nose.

Mama came close and folded one warm wing around him.

"Do you know what I do when it is dark?" she asked.

Miko lifted one ear. "What do you do?"

"I sing," said Mama. "A tiny song. So small and so high that only bats can hear it."

"A song in the dark?" said Miko. "How can a song help me fly?"

"Watch," said Mama. She opened her mouth and made one tiny sound.

Then she was quiet. Very quiet. She listened with her big ears.

"My song flew away," she said. "It touched the wall, and then it came back to me."

"When the song comes home, it tells me where the wall is," said Mama.

Click, click. Listen. The song comes back.

Miko tried. Click. He waited for one blink, then two, and nothing came back.

"Wait a little longer," said Mama. "First you call. Then the song needs a moment to fly."

Miko took a breath and tried again. Click. He waited, very still.

And something soft and small touched his ears. The wall had answered him!

Click, click. Listen. The song comes back.

"Near things answer fast," said Mama. "Far things answer slowly."

Miko clicked at the wall beside him, and the song jumped straight back, quick as a hiccup.

He clicked into the long dark tunnel, and the song came home slowly, like a sleepy yawn.

Now the cave was not empty and black any more. It was full of walls and stones and doorways, and Miko could hear every one.

That night Miko flew out of the cave, and the warm air lifted his wings.

Click. A tall tree. Click. A pond, flat and shiny as a plate. Click, click. A little moth, flapping home.

Miko opened his mouth and caught it, and it was crunchy and good.

The night was wide, and it was full of good things to eat.

Click, click. Listen. The song comes back.

Miko flew on, over the trees and under the big yellow moon.

He sang his little song to everything he passed, just to hear it come home.

Click to a leaf. Click to a stone. Click to a soft grey cloud, far away, that was too soft to answer.

The dark was not empty. The dark was full, and it sang back to him.

When the sky turned soft and pink, Miko flew home to the cave.

He climbed up beside Mama, turned upside down, and wrapped his wings around himself.

"Are you still afraid of the dark?" whispered Mama.

"No," said Miko, and his eyes were nearly closed. "The dark sings back."

Click, click. Listen. The song comes back.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

Miko's "click" is real. A bat calls out and then listens to the echoes bouncing back, building a sound picture of everything around it: how far away a wall is, how big a branch is, where an insect is flying. Near objects return the echo sooner than far ones, which is exactly what Mama teaches him. When a bat closes in on prey it fires a very fast burst of calls, and its ears are tuned to pick its own calls out of the noise.

Two simplifications. First, the "song" is almost entirely above our hearing: bat calls run from about 14,000 Hz to over 100,000 Hz, while people hear up to roughly 20,000 Hz, so a grown-up standing in that cave would hear next to nothing. Second, bats are not blind. They have small, light-sensitive eyes and see well in very dim light; echolocation is an extra sense on top of vision, not a replacement for it. In a pitch-black cave Miko really would depend on echoes, but out under the moon he would be using his eyes as well.

The moth is not a big supper by accident: a little brown bat can catch up to 1,200 mosquito-sized insects in an hour, which is why bats are such good neighbours on a summer evening.
