Noa and the Stars That Blink

Noa is four years old, and every single night she asks one more question before she goes to sleep.

Tonight her dad wraps her up in the soft blue blanket and takes her outside, onto the balcony.

Down below, the city is quiet. Up above, the sky is dark and wide, and all the stars are awake.

Blink, blink, little light. The star is saying good night.

Noa looks up at one small star, right above the tall tree in the garden.

The star shines bright and warm, like a tiny candle very far away.

Then it goes soft, and dim, and Noa can almost not see it any more.

Then it shines again, as bright as it was before.

Noa holds her dad's hand tight. "Dad," she says, "is my star broken?"

Her dad stands very close beside her, and he looks up at the same small star.

"Your star is not broken," he says. "It is not going out, and it is not sick."

"Up there it shines and it shines, all night long, and it never stops for even one second."

"But that little light has a very long way to travel before it gets down here to your eyes."

Noa looks up hard. She sees only dark, quiet sky between her and her star.

"Something is in the way," says Dad. "Something you cannot see at all."

"It is the air. A big, thick blanket of air is wrapped all the way around the world."

"It goes up higher than the birds, and higher than the clouds, and higher than the planes."

"Some of the air is warm, and some of the air is cool, and all of it keeps moving."

"It moves all night long, slowly and quietly, like water in a river."

The little light from the star comes all the way down through that moving air.

Warm air, cool air, warm air, cool air, the whole long way down to the balcony.

Every bit of air bends the light one tiny step this way, and one tiny step that way.

So the light wiggles, and Noa's eyes see it come and go, come and go, come and go.

Blink, blink, little light. The star is saying good night.

"Then why does the moon not blink?" asks Noa.

The moon is sitting above the rooftops, round and calm, like a big plate of light.

"That is a very good question," says Dad, and he smiles down at her.

"Your star is so far away that it looks like one tiny dot, as small as a crumb."

"But the moon is much closer, and much wider. The moon has a great big face."

"On a face that big, the light wiggles in a hundred places at the same time."

"Some of the wiggles go up, and some of the wiggles go down, and they all mix together."

"So the moon stays calm and steady, and it does not blink at all."

Noa watches the moon for a long, long time. It does not blink. Not even once.

"The moon is too big to blink," says Noa, and her dad laughs very quietly.

Dad points down low, at a star that sits just above the dark rooftops.

That one blinks the most of all, fast and busy, like a little lamp in the wind.

"Down there the light has to cross much more air," he says, "so it wiggles much more."

Then Noa finds her own star again, high above them, small and bright and hers.

Blink, blink, little light. The star is saying good night.

Dad carries Noa back inside, and lays her down in her warm, soft bed.

The window is open just a little, and one small star is waiting there in the dark.

It shines, and it goes soft, and it shines again, like a slow and sleepy little wink.

Noa's eyes are very heavy now, and the star keeps on blinking, on and on and on.

"Good night, star," says Noa. And the star blinks back: good night, Noa.

Let's listen: Twinkle, Twinkle

This little tune is very old, and lots of children know it as "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star".

A musician called Mozart liked it so much that he played it in twelve different ways.

This one is the slowest and the softest of them all. Close your eyes and listen.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

Stars really do shine with a steady light. The twinkling is added by our own air: starlight crosses many layers of atmosphere at different temperatures and densities, and because those layers keep moving, they bend (refract) the light a little differently from moment to moment. Astronomers call it scintillation. Above the atmosphere, from a spacecraft, stars do not twinkle at all.

The moon and the planets look steady for the reason Dad gives: they are not points but discs. A star is so far away that it arrives as a single pinpoint, so one wobble of air moves all of its light at once. A disc is wide enough to cover many patches of air at the same time, and those wobbles average out. The same rule explains Dad's last point, which is also true: a star low above the rooftops twinkles more, because its light travels through much more air than a star overhead.

One simplification: "the air bends the light" covers refraction by pockets of turbulent air, and at the eye there is a little diffraction too. And the phrase "a hundred places at the same time" is a picture, not a measurement.
