Sol the Snail and the Silver Trail

Sol is a snail. She is small, and her shell is a little brown swirl she carries on her back.

She lives in the garden by the tall wooden fence, under the big green leaves where it is cool.

Every evening, when the sky turns pink and then grey, Sol comes out for a walk.

She is the slowest walker in the whole garden, and she does not mind that at all.

Slide and stick. Slide and stick. Little by little, bit by bit.

At the very top of the fence there is a flat piece of wood, still warm from the sun.

Sol knows that from up there you can see the first star of the night.

She wants to see it. She wants to see it tonight, before she goes to sleep.

But the fence is tall, and the wood goes straight up, like a wall.

And Sol is slow. Very, very slow.

Sol put her soft foot on the wood and began to climb, slowly, slowly.

Up she went. One little bit. Two little bits. Three little bits.

Then her shell felt heavy, and she wobbled, and she slid all the way back down.

Sol sat in the cool grass and did not want to try again.

Maybe snails are not made for climbing walls, she thought.

Then something shiny came along the path. It was Nana Snail.

Nana is old and big, and soft green moss grows on her round brown shell.

"Why are you sitting in the grass, little Sol?" she asked.

"I want to see the first star, up on top of the fence," said Sol.

"But I am too slow, and I slip, and I always come down again."

Nana smiled. "Turn around," she said. "Look behind you."

Sol turned her head, and there on the ground was a long, shiny line.

It went past the leaves and past the stones, all the way from her house.

It was silver in the moonlight, and it was hers. She had made it herself.

"That is your slime," said Nana. "It is the best thing you have."

"Your slime does two jobs at the same time," said Nana.

"When you hold still, it holds you tight to the wood, like glue."

"When you push with your foot, it turns slippery, and you slide."

"Sticky. Slippery. Sticky. Slippery. That is how a little snail climbs a wall."

Sol looked up at the fence again. It did not look so tall now.

Sol tried it on a smooth grey stone, close to the ground.

She pushed her foot, and she slid. She stopped, and she stuck.

She hung on the side of the stone and she did not fall down.

"Oh!" said Sol. "My slime is a rope and a slide, both together."

Nana nodded her old mossy shell. "Now go and see your star."

Sol put her foot back on the wood and pushed, very gently.

This time she did not hurry. She stopped, she held on, and she pushed again.

Behind her she left a shiny silver ribbon, right up the middle of the post.

"Slow is fine, Sol," called Nana from down below. "Slow gets there too."

Slide and stick. Slide and stick. Little by little, bit by bit.

Up she went, past a wide green leaf. Up past a round grey nail.

A little wind came and pushed at her shell, but Sol did not fall.

Her own slime held her to the wood, tight and safe, all the way.

Slide and stick. Slide and stick. Little by little, bit by bit.

Under her, the garden grew smaller and smaller, and quieter and quieter.

And then the wood ended, and Sol was standing on top of the fence.

The sky was dark blue and soft, and there, high above the tree, was the first star.

Sol looked at it for a long, long time, and the star seemed to look back.

Then she pulled into her shell, warm and round, with her silver trail behind her.

Slide and stick. Slide and stick. Good night, little Sol.

Before we sleep

  1. What would you do instead of climbing, if you wanted to see the first star tonight?
  2. Sol is the slowest walker in the garden. What is something you like to do slowly?

Tomorrow you can put a little water on a smooth plate and slide one finger through it. Press down and hold: your finger sticks. Push along: it slides. That is Sol's trick, in your own hand.

Good night

Sol holds the wood, the wood holds the fence, and your bed holds you until morning.

For grown-ups: what is true here, and sources

The true part: a snail's slime really does two opposite jobs at once. Pedal mucus is viscoelastic – at rest it behaves like a solid glue that grips the surface, and under the shear of the muscle waves running along the foot it yields and flows like a lubricant, then heals again. That is why a snail can hold on to a vertical fence, or a wall, or the underside of a leaf, and still glide forward.

Sol's slowness is honest. Most snails move less than about 8 cm a minute, so a real climb up a fence post takes many patient minutes – "little by little" is not a figure of speech. Garden snails are genuinely night creatures and do climb fences, walls and plant stems after dark, especially when the air is damp.

Two things are story licence: snails do not set out to look at stars, and their slime is not a rope they choose to use – it is simply always there, doing both jobs. The trail is also a real map: snails and slugs follow existing slime trails, which saves them energy, since making mucus is expensive.
